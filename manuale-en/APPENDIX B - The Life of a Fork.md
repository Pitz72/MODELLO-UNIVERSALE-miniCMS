# APPENDIX B: The Life of a Fork

The Model describes how a thin-stack site is built. It doesn’t say what happens when you **fork** one, and yet that’s a real event in the life of these projects, and we have one example mapped from the inside: FDCA, the “Festival della Canzone Artificiale” (Festival of the Artificial Song), is born as a fork of DISINTELLIGENZA, the “Festival della Disintelligenza Naturale” (Festival of Natural Disintelligence). Same author, same engine, new theme: from the comedy of human error to generative music. This appendix tells that phase, because it has risks all its own, and the first is the most silent of all.

Chapter 2 already introduced the pattern: two festivals with the same functional base start from a copy of what already works. The advantage is independence; the risk is its exact flip side. Here we look at it up close.

---

## 1. The Backend Freezes, the Frontend Restarts

The first thing you notice about FDCA is where the fork’s energy went. The `public/api/` folder is copied from DISINTELLIGENZA **byte for byte**: the same 28 files, the same logic for authentication, voting, upload, newsletter, festival, zero differences in content. All the new work is on the public *shell*: rewritten showcase pages (Home, Philosophy, Manifesto, Past Editions, Contacts), a different branding, an animated cursor. It’s the natural pattern of a re-skin: you change what you see, not what works.

The consequence is that the server logic is inherited intact. And “intact” includes everything, the flaws too.

---

## 2. The Fork Inherits the Debt: Copying an Insecure Backend Multiplies It

This is the central point of the appendix. None of DISINTELLIGENZA’s security vulnerabilities was fixed in the fork, because the backend is copied line by line. What was a problem on one site becomes the same identical problem on two.

| Inherited vulnerability | Where it’s described | Status in FDCA |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Public-upload RCE chain** (no-auth upload, MIME from the client, name preserved, no PHP-off) | Ch. 7 | present, unchanged |
| **Base-rung auth** (no CSRF, no rate limit, no fixation protection) | Ch. 10 | present |
| **Newsletter with no double opt-in or token** + header injection | Ch. 13 | present |
| **Destructive resets with no CSRF token** | Ch. 10, Ch. 14 | present |
| **Denormalized `vote_count`** (ranking drift) | Ch. 18 | present |
| **Render without DOMPurify** (stored XSS) | Ch. 8 | present |

There’s a detail that makes the story even more instructive. On DISINTELLIGENZA’s repository there’s an open intervention to close the upload’s RCE chain (Ch. 7). That fix lives on DISINTELLIGENZA, and **doesn’t touch FDCA**: the fork has its own copy of the vulnerable file, disconnected from the original. The day FDCA’s backend goes online, the RCE will be replicated, identical, on a second domain, while everyone believes they’ve solved it.

> [!WARNING]
> **The fix doesn’t follow the fork**
> When you fork by copying the backend, you inherit the features and you inherit the flaws, in the same gesture. From that moment the two branches are independent: every security fix made on one **doesn’t** reach the other by magic, it has to be reapplied by hand. It’s easy to forget, because the fork looks like “another project,” with another name and another version. But underneath it’s the same code, and a flaw closed on one branch stays wide open on the other until someone closes it again, on purpose. Before forking a backend, it’s worth having a list of its known vulnerabilities precisely so as not to lose sight of it after the copy.

---

## 3. The Disconnected Shell

There’s a second surprise in FDCA: the new frontend **doesn’t talk to the inherited backend**. There’s no `src/api.ts`, not a single `fetch` call to `/api/`. The showcase pages are pure marketing, not connected to the CMS running behind them. FDCA, as it stands, is a showcase site with a full, working CMS behind it that’s unreachable from its own application.

It isn’t a mistake: it’s a phase. The skin first, then (maybe) the rewiring. It’s the disconnection typical of a restyle in progress, and on its own it does no harm, because a backend no one calls exposes nothing. The risk is latent and deferred: the day the frontend gets connected, it will inherit *operationally too* the behaviors, and the bugs, of DISINTELLIGENZA’s backend. Today’s disconnection is also the reason the flaws in §2 aren’t yet exploitable end-to-end, and the reason it’s easy to forget about them.

---

## 4. The Version That Restarts from Zero on Code That Doesn’t

FDCA presents itself as **v0.0.1**. The `package.json` says 0.0.1, the `metadata.json` talks about a “Remix,” the README is the one generated by Google AI Studio. Everything signals a new product, just born. Underneath, though, runs a backend at v0.5.x with all its accumulated debt.

> [!WARNING]
> **The version tells of a birth, the code tells of an inheritance**
> The version discontinuity *hides* the continuity of the debt. It’s the reverse of the divergent version-strings problem you see in DISINTELLIGENZA (the sidebar, init, and package show three different numbers): there too many versions for a single codebase, here a version-zero for code that’s anything but zero. In both cases the version number lies about the real state of the software. When you see a `0.0.1`, don’t take for granted that there’s little underneath: look at the code, not the label.

---

## 5. The Fork That Writes Its Own Roadmap

FDCA includes a `ROADMAP-EVOLUZIONE-miniCMS` folder with nine chapters, AI-assisted, that plan to rebuild the miniCMS cleanly. The titles of those chapters **trace the same clusters** as this very mapping: Backend, PHP API, Frontend Bridge, Admin & Protected Routes, Festival Engine. It’s a precious artifact, because it confirms from the inside the structure this manual reconstructs from the outside: the backbone of the thin stack is recognizable even to those who work inside it, to the point of wanting to rewrite it in order. A project that documents the intention to evolve its own engine is the proof that the pattern exists and is legible.

---

## 6. One Engine, Two Festivals

There’s a bright side to forking too, and FDCA demonstrates it. The festival model (sign-up, selection, round-based voting, registration and voting master switches, a ranking derived from `vote_count`) is **the same** on the two sites: only the theme changes, from human error to generative music. It’s the positive proof that the contest module described in Chapters 17 and 18 is a genuinely reusable component: the same engine carries two different festivals with a simple re-skin.

Forking, in short, isn’t a bad thing in itself. Reusing a domain that works by changing the skin is efficient and legitimate. It becomes a problem only when the re-skin forgets the engine: when you copy the backend and overlook that inside that copy there were the flaws too, and that from then on it’s the fork’s responsibility to keep them closed.

---

## In Summary

Forking a thin-stack project is a real and common phase, and it has a physiology of its own: the backend freezes, the frontend restarts, the version resets, the theme changes. The specific risk is just one, but it’s serious: the silent inheritance of the debt. A fork that copies the backend verbatim carries every vulnerability along with it, unchanged, while the version-zero and the new name tell of a clean product. The fix doesn’t follow the fork: it has to be reapplied by hand, on a branch that now lives a life of its own. The rule, if you fork, is to keep two lists together: the one of the features to reuse and the one of the flaws not to inherit. The first is the reason you fork; the second is the reason a fork has to be treated as a project to secure from scratch, not as a copy already in order.
